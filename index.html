Keeping a certification means keeping count.

Most professional certifications lapse unless you earn continuing education every cycle. Each certifying body counts it its own way, and a plain sum of hours is usually wrong in the direction that costs you the credential. These tools do the counting.

This page is a working guide to continuing education credits: how they work, how ten bodies count them, what to keep for an audit and what usually doesn't count. Registrar, the free tracker further down, does the arithmetic. Open Registrar or read on.

The problem

Security, fraud, privacy and project-management certifications ask you to keep learning: so many credits every one to four years, under a name that changes from body to body (CPE, CEU, PDU). The rules behind that number are where people get caught:

  • Caps. Some kinds of activity count only up to a limit per cycle. Hours past the cap are logged, but they are not credit.
  • Minimums. A share of the total must come from the core subject of the credential. General hours cannot make up for it.
  • Windows and deadlines. Some bodies want credits reported each year by a fixed date; others want the whole total at the end, sometimes with a grace period, usually with a fee.
  • Evidence. Audits ask for certificates long after the event. A folder of PDFs you cannot match to entries is not much help.

Example. A CISSP needs 120 CPEs in three years, at least 90 of them in Group A (the CISSP domains), with Group B (general development) counting for at most 30. Log 80 hours of domain training and 40 of general development and a spreadsheet says 120 of 120. The real count is 110: ten Group B hours are forfeited, and Group A is 10 short. More general hours will not fix that.

Body rules change; your body's own portal is always the authority. More on CPE rules.

The two-minute version

If you have just passed your first certification exam, this is the part to read. Every body has its own vocabulary, but the mechanics are much the same everywhere.

  • You earn credits by learning, and you need a set number per cycle. The cycle is one to four years depending on the body. Some bodies also set a floor for every year inside the cycle; most only care about the total at the end.
  • One hour, one credit, usually. Most bodies award one credit per hour of qualifying activity. Not all of them: ISACA's CPE hour is 50 minutes, and EC-Council's policy does not tie a credit to a clock hour at all. Find out before you round anything up.
  • Domain credits and general credits. Most bodies separate learning tied to the credential's subject from general professional development. ISC2 calls them Group A (activity in your certification's domains) and Group B (the skills around the skills: leadership, communication, management). The split matters because general credits are usually capped and domain credits usually have a floor.
  • Credits reach your record in one of three ways. Some providers report them for you: at ISC2, its own events and its listed CPE Partners can submit on your behalf, which is why conferences ask for your member number at registration. Others give you a certificate of attendance to upload yourself. Everything else (a book, a podcast, a paper, a chapter meeting) you claim yourself, with whatever documentation you have and a short summary of what you learned. ISC2 asks for that summary on Group A claims.
  • One activity can count more than once. ISC2 applies credits it adds to your account to all your ISC2 certifications in their active cycle (not to one that is in its grace period). EC-Council applies credits across all the EC-Council certifications you hold. Between bodies, the same certificate can usually support a claim at each, if each body's rules accept that kind of activity. You still report it to each body separately.

Habits that save you

  • Log it the week it happens. Not in the month your cycle ends. A week later you still know the title, the hours, the provider and where the certificate went. Two years later you do not, and two years later is when the audit letter arrives.
  • Keep one evidence folder. Every certificate, receipt, agenda and confirmation email in one place, named so you can match each file to its entry. Bodies audit, and "I know I went" is not evidence.
  • Read your body's handbook once. Cycle lengths, annual minimums, caps and categories differ from body to body, and they change. Read the current maintenance policy end to end once, and again when the body announces a change. For ISC2 that is the member policies; the table below links every other body's page.
  • Grace periods are parachutes, not plans. Many bodies give you a little time after the cycle ends: ISC2 90 days, CompTIA 30. That time often comes with a fee or a suspended credential, and some bodies state no grace period at all. Plan to finish without it.
  • Touch the system weekly. A spreadsheet, a folder of PDFs, a portal, this tool: the only tracking system that works is the one you open every week. Ten minutes on a Friday is enough.

Audit-ready records

An audit is a request to prove what you claimed. It can come years after the activity, and you usually get a fixed window to answer (ACFE gives 60 days). For each activity, keep:

  • The certificate or confirmation. A certificate of attendance, a completion email, a transcript line or a registration receipt with the agenda. Something with your name on it, from someone other than you.
  • The date. The day it happened, not the day you logged it. It decides which cycle, and which year, the credit belongs to.
  • The hours. And how you got them: the session times on the agenda, not the length of the day.
  • The provider. Who ran it, and their provider number if your body uses one.
  • One line on what you learned. It turns a self-claim into evidence, and it is what ISC2 asks for on Group A claims. Write it the same week, while you remember.
  • Where you claimed it. Which credential, which category, and the date you reported it.

Keep the records for as long as your body says, counted from the right date: ACFE 3 years, PMI 18 months after the cycle ends, ISACA 12 months after the cycle ends. Keeping everything for the life of the credential is simpler and costs nothing.

Common surprises: what usually doesn't count

None of these is universal. Each is a rule at enough bodies to catch people out, so treat every one as a question for your own body.

  • Doing your day job. Ordinary work rarely earns credit, and where it does it is capped low: CompTIA counts work experience up to 9, GIAC up to 12, PMI up to 8 per cycle for PMP. Check your body's rule.
  • Hours past a cap. They stay in your log and they are not credit. Ten extra webinars do not help once the category is full. Check your body's rule.
  • General hours where a domain floor applies. The CISSP example above: no amount of Group B fills a Group A gap. Check your body's rule.
  • The whole conference day. Credit is for the sessions, not for travel, meals, breaks or the expo floor. A nine-to-five day is rarely eight credits. Check your body's rule.
  • Sales pitches. A product demo or a sponsor session that is mostly marketing is often excluded, or questioned at audit. Check your body's rule.
  • The same thing twice. Repeating a course you already claimed, or claiming one activity in two cycles. EC-Council, for one, lets an activity count in only one window. Check your body's rule.
  • The wrong cycle. Credit usually belongs to the cycle in which the activity happened. Carryover, where it exists, is limited (ACFE up to 10; PMI only from the final 12 months). Check your body's rule.
  • Rounding up. Forty-five minutes is not an hour, and at ISACA a CPE hour is 50 minutes. Some bodies accept fractions, some round down. Check your body's rule.
  • A claim with nothing behind it. A self-claim with no documentation can be disallowed at audit, and the credit goes with it. Check your body's rule.

Your first 90 days with a new cert

  1. Week one: find your dates. In the body's portal, note the cycle start and end, the total, any annual minimum and when the maintenance fee is due. Work out when the grace period, if there is one, would end, and then forget it. Make the evidence folder now, while it is empty.
  2. First month: read the rules. Read the maintenance handbook once. Write down the caps and floors that apply to you. Give your member number to the providers you use, so the ones that report credits for you can. Turn on two-factor sign-in where the portal offers it.
  3. By day 90: set a pace and a habit. Divide the total by the months in the cycle: 120 over 36 months is about 3.3 a month; 20 a year is under 2 a month. Pick one steady source (a chapter meeting, a monthly webinar series) and put a ten-minute weekly reminder in your calendar to log what you did.

Where to earn credits

This list leans toward insider threat and investigations work, where nearly everything you learn counts somewhere: the field sits between security, behavioural science, law and investigations. Most of it serves any security credential.

  • NITSIG (National Insider Threat Special Interest Group). Free membership and the largest network of insider threat practitioners: meetings in the Washington, DC area (JHU-APL in Laurel, and Herndon), webinars, and the annual Insider Threat Symposium & Expo. The most insider-threat-dense hours you can log.
  • CISA Insider Threat Mitigation. Free federal resources, training and self-assessment tools.
  • CMU SEI / CERT. The research group behind much of the field's foundational work, with paid courses and certificates for program builders and analysts.
  • CInRM CoE (Canadian Insider Risk Management Centre of Excellence). Practical certificates in insider risk management (screening and risk factors, investigations, organizational integration, OSINT) that build toward a professional certificate, developed with Carleton University's Norman Paterson School of International Affairs. Each practical certificate carries 12 ASIS-recognized CPE credits.
  • SecureWorld. Regional conferences worth 6 to 18 CPEs depending on the pass, and free hour-long Remote Sessions webcasts with downloadable certificates.
  • GuidePoint GPSEC. Free one-day regional conferences with insider threat content. Give your ISC2 number at registration and the credits post for you. The monthly Brick House webinar adds steady hours.
  • Gartner. Free research webinars, plus the Security & Risk Management and IAM summits. Not an official ISC2 provider: ask for the certificate of attendance and submit it yourself.
  • FS-ISAC for the financial sector, and your local ISC2 and ISACA chapters. Member events and monthly chapter meetings are quiet, steady sources of credit.

Give some back. Presenting, publishing and volunteering earn credit at most bodies. Preparing a conference talk counts; so does writing for, or maintaining, a community resource. Mind the caps: PMI, for example, counts Giving Back up to 25 of the PMP's 60.

Provider details were current when the source page was checked in July 2026. Offerings change: confirm eligibility with the provider and with your body.

Glossary

CPE
Continuing Professional Education. The unit at ISC2, ISACA, GIAC, EC-Council, IAPP and ACFE. Usually one per hour of activity; at ISACA, one per 50 minutes.
CEU
Continuing Education Unit. CompTIA's name for its credit. In some other fields a CEU means ten contact hours, so a CEU at one body is not automatically one credit at another.
PDU
Professional Development Unit. PMI's unit, one per hour; the University of Maryland uses the same name for GCITP.
CPD
Continuing Professional Development. The general term, common in the UK and the Commonwealth, and the name some bodies give their whole scheme.
Cycle total
The number of credits you need by the end of the cycle (one to four years).
Annual minimum
A floor you must meet every year inside the cycle, whatever your total looks like. ISACA (20 a year) and ACFE (20, on a one-year cycle) have one; ISC2 has one only for Associates.
Group A and Group B
ISC2's split between credits in your certification's domains (A) and general professional development (B). Other bodies make the same distinction under other names.
Cap and floor
A cap is the most a category can contribute; a floor is the least a category must contribute.
Carryover
Credits beyond the requirement moved into the next cycle. Some bodies allow a limited amount, some none.
Grace period
Time after the cycle ends to finish earning or reporting, often with a fee or a suspended credential. Not every body has one.
Audit
A request to document what you claimed: certificates, dates, hours, providers. Usually random, sometimes years later.

Who it is for

  • People who hold one or more certifications and renew them themselves: security analysts, investigators, fraud examiners, threat-assessment and insider-risk professionals.
  • Anyone holding two credentials whose cycles, units and caps do not line up, who wants one record that answers "am I on track for each?"
  • People who would rather not hand their training history and certificates to another online account.

Early release. Registrar works today, but it is young. The browser version keeps everything on your device; there is no hosted account version. Your data stays in your browser

  • The browser app has no account and no server copy. Your activities, settings and certificates are stored in your browser, on your device, and nowhere else.
  • Certificates are read on your device. The PDF reader and the text-recognition engine come from this site, not from a third-party server, and the site's security policy stops the app from talking to any other site.
  • There are no analytics, no tracking and no cookies. Nothing is backed up for you either: clearing site data or losing the device loses the ledger, so use Export now and then.
  • One optional feature sends data out: if you add your own AI provider key, the text read from one certificate goes to that provider when you ask. Never the file, and the key is never saved.
  • Registrar never asks for, stores or uses your certifying body's portal login. You submit to your body yourself.
